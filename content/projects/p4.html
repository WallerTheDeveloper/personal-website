VR Tower Defense puts the game in the room you are actually standing in. You put the headset on and the room is still there — your floor, your table, your walls — and then enemies start walking across it towards the base you are defending.

You defend it by reaching out with your bare hands and setting gun turrets down on the real surfaces around you. There is nothing to hold. The headset watches your hands.

Portfolio project — built for the Meta Quest 3 as a personal technical showcase, to find out what happens to a very familiar kind of game when the board is a real room and the only controller is your hands.

How you play it

  1. Turn your palm towards your face. A small menu appears on your wrist holding the towers you can build. Turn your hand away and it is gone again — there is no button for it and nothing floating in the room waiting to be dismissed.
  2. Pinch the one you want. Reach across with your other hand and pinch it, the same gesture you would use to pick up anything small. That is the whole selection mechanic.
  3. Carry it out and let go. Put it down wherever you think it will do the most good. It settles flat and square onto whatever you set it on, so a turret placed on the edge of a table ends up standing on the table rather than sinking through it or leaning off it.
  4. Stand back. Once it is down it works on its own — watching for enemies coming into range, choosing which one matters most, and opening fire. Your job is deciding what goes where, and then living with it.

What is in it

◈ Three kinds of tower

A rocket launcher that hits hard and hits everything nearby, a machine gun that fires fast and steadily, and a laser that holds a continuous beam on one target. They are worth different things in different places, which is what makes placement a decision.

⤢ A menu worn on the wrist

Your inventory lives on your arm rather than on a panel hanging in space. You check it the way you would check a watch, and it stays out of the way the rest of the time.

◎ Towers that place themselves properly

Aiming a virtual object onto a real surface by hand is fiddly and unforgiving. Here you only have to be roughly right — the tower works out the surface it has landed on and aligns itself to it.

⟐ Hands, not controllers

Reaching, grabbing, pinching and letting go are read from your actual hands. Nothing has to be picked up before you can start and nothing runs out of battery mid-wave.

△ A base worth defending

Enemies advance on your headquarters and damage it if they arrive. Everything you have put down between them and it is the only thing in their way.

▤ Legible at a glance

Health bars over the things that can be hurt, and muzzle flashes, beams and impacts you can read from across the room without stopping to look. In a headset you cannot pause to study a number.

Why it had to be hands

A tower defense game is normally played by clicking a map from above. Here you are inside it at your own height, so the decisions change shape: you crouch to see what a turret can actually see, and you find out that the far corner of your own living room is a blind spot. Placing a tower by physically putting it somewhere makes that a spatial judgement rather than a menu one — which is the entire reason for building it in a headset instead of on a screen.

What you need to run it

A Meta Quest 3 and a bit of clear floor. No controllers, no extra hardware, no setting up the room first.

Status — a finished prototype. The loop is complete and playable end to end: build, place, defend.
